# Why changing shape needs a flight path

Suppose four drones hold the corners of a plane. An operator commands a pyramid while they are airborne. A template gives four destination points, but it does not say which drone should take each point. Drone IDs cannot be relabeled to make a plot look good: the actual vehicles must move along physical paths.

The ground-to-template assignment from construction is a poor choice for this new move. Two nominal plane-to-pyramid paths cross at the same point halfway through, even though both endpoint formations have separated slots. ALiGn's transition planner tries all 24 destination assignments for four drones. For each pair it computes the closest approach during **synchronized straight-line interpolation**. If relative position is `r(α)=r₀+αv`, the minimum squared distance occurs at `α=clamp(−r₀·v/|v|², 0, 1)`. The planner keeps assignments with at least 0.55 m clearance and chooses the shortest total travel. The accepted narrow plan assigned slots `[1, 2, 3, 0]` and had 0.913 m ideal-path clearance. This is geometry, not a safety guarantee: real drones move asynchronously and respond to downwash.

The simulator reference changes actor-visible relative targets and reward targets at step 2,600, clears progress and smoothing memory for the new target, resets success dwell, and keeps drone identities fixed. The first two narrow-pyramid flights issued the command in all four worlds but timed out. Raising the destination center so the three base targets stayed at 1.5 m did not fix it. In the raised flight, one lower drone had begun falling by step 2,800 and reached the ground after the apex climbed above it. The source multirotor model applies a downward aerodynamic interaction called **downwash** from a higher drone to lower drones. A position-based proxy for that model rose to about 0.310 in both failed narrow flights. It is not a force measurement or proof of causation.

A controlled geometry test kept the raised center and doubled only the destination's horizontal offsets. The model's nominal endpoint exposure factor fell from 0.2177 to 0.0108. That flight completed in all four near-identical deterministic worlds 5.51 s after the command, with about 1.000 m minimum separation, 0.0448 m final-100-step assigned error, and no post-command contact. The exposure proxy on its recorded positions peaked at 0.0171. This supports the interference hypothesis, but the wider shape also changes travel geometry, so it does not isolate force causality.

The first success required only two seconds of stable holding. A separate 10-second dwell run remained airborne and contact-free but timed out in all four worlds: the base drones settled about 0.147 m below target, and the final assigned error was 0.1275 m, above the 0.1 m tolerance. A one-variable reference-controller gain test is prepared to check that steady offset. Neither deterministic reference result establishes that a learned actor can respond to a shape command, and the four worlds are not independent training seeds.
