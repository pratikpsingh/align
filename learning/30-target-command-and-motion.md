# From a target to measured motion

A drone can be assigned a formation slot without ever reaching it. To find where that chain breaks, compare four quantities in one time step: **target position**, **actor action**, **commanded velocity**, and **measured next state**. The actor outputs four bounded numbers. The first three specify a direction; the magnitude of the fourth sets speed. For example, action `(1, 0, 0, 0.5)` with a 0.5 m/s maximum commands +x at 0.25 m/s. The Lee controller then chooses rotor signals. The simulator advances physics and reports a new position and velocity. A correct action-to-command calculation does not guarantee the drone moves +x immediately: rotor response, inertia, resets, and contact matter.

[Evaluation instrumentation](../src/align/simulation/policy_evaluation.py) saves the pre-step state, action, controller command, rotor outputs, post-step state, and assigned target for each drone. The optional `--policy-telemetry` flag keeps ordinary evaluation files unchanged. [The host audit](../src/align/tasks/policy_telemetry.py) recomputes the expected command, reward sums, and formation geometry from the saved rows. This catches a logging or frame mismatch before we explain a policy's behavior from the trace.

The reward trace distinguishes **raw terms** from **weighted contributions**. For example, a raw formation value of `-1`, formation weight `1`, and 0.01-second control step contributes `-0.01` to that drone's reward. Summing its eight weighted terms must equal its per-drone reward; averaging drone rewards must equal the team reward. The audit checks both equations. If formation error stays high while the weighted formation term is tiny relative to other terms, that suggests a reward-scale question; it does not prove that increasing the weight will fix control. A controlled ablation would test that.

The initial bounded replay uses an immutable seed-41, update-12 plane-specialist checkpoint. It trains nothing and records the source checkpoint hash. Its success criteria are a passing simulator probe, exact per-drone row count, zero optimizer updates, and a passing CPU trace audit. After live data exists, inspect the formation-phase time series around the takeoff-to-formation target switch and decide whether to test a controller correction, reward change, or task design change. [The run instructions](../docs/31-policy-telemetry.md) give the exact command and artifacts. Per-step velocity alignment is only descriptive: one short interval cannot establish tracking quality or safety.

## What the accepted flight taught us

The [passed replay](../docs/31-policy-telemetry.md#accepted-lab-replay-and-cpu-analysis) logged all 12,800 drone steps and reproduced the source checkpoint's aggregate metrics exactly. The CPU analysis is saved under `runs/policy-telemetry-analysis/20260918T130838.847388IST-ea74ac9d/` (runs are ignored by Git). In the formation interval, only 45.8% of commands pointed toward each drone's assigned target. The drones moved at about 0.086 m/s on average while commanding about 0.095 m/s. That makes an action-selection and task-timing explanation more plausible than a gross wrong-axis command conversion, but it does not prove the inner controller is calibrated over all conditions.

Here is a simple deadline calculation. Formation begins at recorded step 551. With an 800-step episode and a required 200 consecutive settled steps, settling must begin by step 601: 50 control intervals remain. Each interval is 0.01 s; at the 0.5 m/s command ceiling, the greatest commanded travel is `50 × 0.01 × 0.5 = 0.25 m`. The drones were still about 1.2–1.3 m below their target altitude when formation began. Even an ideal command at the ceiling would leave about a metre of altitude error at the dwell deadline. The report computes a roughly 1.01 m lower bound for assigned RMSE using only altitude, compared with a 0.10 m success threshold. This is conditional on the velocity-command model; it is not a hard physical speed law.

The next experiment should separate **insufficient time** from **weak learned commands**. Keep the checkpoint fixed and alter only a declared evaluation timing condition, then measure whether takeoff altitude, formation error, separation, and success improve. A reward-weight change is a separate experiment because the observed tracking contribution (`−0.0250` per formation drone step) and formation contribution (`−0.00492`) alone do not show which reward will train a better policy.
